Decode · composition of optimizations · November 2026

fdec: decode optimizations, measured together.

fdec, short for fight decode, is the knlp method for combining decode optimizations and showing that the combination is safe. Each optimization is a brick that reduces one pool of bytes a decode step reads. A brick counts only after it is measured together with every other brick, on text that no calibration step has seen.

A decode step on a GPU at low batch is limited by the bytes it moves, not by arithmetic. Those bytes are in separate pools: the decoder-block weights, the key/value (KV) cache and the output head. Each pool has its own best reducer, so no single technique wins decode. The useful result is a set of reducers that work together, with a measurement that shows they do not damage each other. One command runs that measurement.

Result: int8 weights, an asymmetric KV cache and a certified output head compose in all eight combinations on Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen3-8B. Together they remove 40% to 46% of the bytes a decode step reads at a 32,768-token context, at a perplexity cost of 0.14% to 0.26%; the 95% intervals of the cost end below 0.5%. On GSM8K and MMLU the full stack answers as many questions as the 16-bit model within the resolution of the test: the 95% interval of the difference in accuracy contains zero for all three models on both tasks. The certified head returns the exact token in every cell. A trained cartridge composes with int8 weights and the asymmetric cache at a combined loss cost of 0.1%. Two earlier results did not survive stricter measurement: a published byte saving was in-sample, and the first matrix rounded attention scores to 16 bits. See the corrections.

01 / Why several bricks

Three byte pools, and no pool has a fixed share.

For each generated token the GPU reads every weight in the decoder blocks once, reads the whole KV cache, and multiplies the hidden state against the output head. At low batch the arithmetic is small next to the memory traffic, so latency follows bytes read divided by memory bandwidth. The memory-traffic study gives the evidence for that.

Decoder blocksKV cacheOutput head

Bytes read per decode step at a 32,768-token context, batch 1, from the byte model in the harness. The input embedding is left out because a step reads one row of it.

The KV share grows with context and with batch. The head share grows with vocabulary size. On Qwen2.5-7B the decoder blocks are 81% of the bytes; on Llama-3.2-3B they are 55% and the KV cache is 37%. A reducer for one pool has a ceiling set by that pool's share, so the stack needs one reducer per pool.

There are two kinds of optimization. Byte movement bricks reduce what a step reads: quantized weights, a quantized or shorter KV cache, a partial read of the head. Arithmetic intensity bricks do more useful work per byte read, for example a mixed-precision attention kernel that runs on Tensor Cores. The matrix on this page measures the byte movement bricks. Speed is a separate measurement.

The weight brick is int8 and not int4. The Fisher-sensitivity study that ranked which weights need more bits found int8 to be the point where quality holds without calibration. Int4 with activation-aware or second-order error compensation looked good on a six-prompt proxy and lost quality on a held-out evaluation, so it is not in the stack.

The KV brick keeps keys at 16 bits because keys are what break. Symmetric FP8 keys collapse on models with a large key bias (FP8 failure atlas).

Linear-attention models such as Gated DeltaNet, Mamba2 and RWKV remove the KV pool instead of shrinking it. They are a model class and not a brick. The KV and cartridge bricks do not apply to them, and the weight and head bricks are not yet measured on them.

03 / The rule

What "composes" means.

A cell is one model with one subset of bricks enabled. The harness measures every subset, so three bricks give eight cells. A cell composes when three conditions hold.

1. It runs

A shape, data-type or code-path conflict is recorded as a failed cell, not as a crash.

2. Exactness claims still hold

The certified head must return the token that the full output head selects, computed in float32, at every evaluated position of that cell's own model. It is checked on hidden states that the other bricks have already changed.

3. Quality costs add

The increase in log-perplexity of the cell minus the sum of the increases of its bricks measured alone is the interaction term. The upper end of its 95% interval must be at most 0.01. A larger term means the bricks amplify each other's error, and an evaluation that is too small to resolve the limit does not pass.

Held-out text only

Quality is measured against the 16-bit model on WikiText-2 test: perplexity, top-1 agreement of the predicted token, and mean KL divergence. A brick that needs calibration takes it from WikiText-2 train.

The certified head is exact in every cell that has it, including the cells where int8 weights and FP8 values changed the hidden states it certifies. A screen built from the 16-bit model reads the same fraction of the head as one rebuilt for the quantized model, so the head artifact does not need a rebuild when the other bricks change.

Only int8 weights and the asymmetric cache change the logits, so each model has one interaction term in perplexity: the cost of the two bricks together minus the sum of their costs alone. The certified head returns the exact token and changes no logit, so its check is exactness and not perplexity.

Each 95% interval takes the 16 evaluation sequences of 512 tokens as the observations, because the positions of one sequence share a context. The limit for the interaction term is 0.01.

Every interval of the interaction term contains zero, and its ends are within 0.0024 of zero, a quarter of the limit. The evaluation therefore resolves the limit: an interaction of 0.01 would be far outside each interval. Since commit 3fcdc066b the rule compares the upper end of the interval with the limit, and every cell of this run passes it. The full stack costs 0.14% to 0.26% in perplexity, and the intervals of that cost end at 0.33% to 0.48%. An earlier run at commit 0d5fe72b6 on the same GPU gives the same perplexity, top-1 agreement and KL in every cell, in all digits. The fraction of the head that the certified decode reads differs by at most 0.02 points between the two runs.

Top-1 agreement is the more sensitive measure. Each brick alone changes about 2% of the predicted tokens, and the full stack changes 2% to 3%, while perplexity moves by at most 0.26%. The changed predictions are therefore near-ties. The next section checks what that does to answers.

The FP8 cast of the values

The asymmetric cache casts each value to FP8 as it is. That is the default of the vLLM FP8 cache, so the cell computes what a serving engine computes. The first version of these tables divided each value tensor by one scale before the cast, which uses the whole FP8 range. With that scale the cells that contain the cache have a log-perplexity 0.0001 to 0.0012 lower and a top-1 agreement within 0.001, every cell composes, and the full stack is within 0.14% of the 16-bit perplexity. The cells without the cache are identical in the two runs. --kv-scale tensor selects this variant. Both variants were also measured on one RTX 4090 with PyTorch 2.10, for Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B. Every cell composes there, and the two GPUs agree within 0.008 in perplexity and 0.004 in top-1 agreement in every cell. With the direct cast, the full stack on the RTX 4090 is within 0.17% and 0.10% of the 16-bit perplexity of the two models.

05 / Task accuracy

Do the bricks change answers?

On three models, 26 of 30 paired comparisons show no difference in task accuracy between a cell with bricks and the 16-bit model: the 95% interval of the difference contains zero. With all three bricks, MMLU accuracy is 56.77% against 56.57% on Llama-3.2-3B, 74.17% against 74.25% on Qwen2.5-7B and 75.05% against 74.96% on Qwen3-8B. GSM8K accuracy with all three bricks is 29.3% against 29.4% on Llama-3.2-3B, 81.0% against 81.1% on Qwen2.5-7B and 89.4% against 88.9% on Qwen3-8B. The interval of each of these six differences contains zero.

Four of the 30 intervals exclude zero: two losses and two gains. On Qwen2.5-7B, int8 weights with the asymmetric cache lose 0.21 points of MMLU accuracy, and the certified head alone loses 1.29 points of GSM8K accuracy. On Qwen3-8B, int8 weights alone gain 1.36 points of GSM8K accuracy and the certified head alone gains 0.83 points. None of the four is significant after a correction for the 30 comparisons. A test of each table as a whole finds that the cells of the two GSM8K tables of the Qwen models differ among themselves: the lowest and the highest cell are 2.35 and 1.82 points apart, and the 16-bit cell is between them.

Control runs test these differences, each with a rule set before the run. The three controls for Qwen2.5-7B are done, and none reproduces its difference. The controls for Qwen3-8B are running. Their first result is mixed: the gain of int8 weights returns with one other int8 group size and not with the other. The table gives the state of each control.

Perplexity and top-1 agreement are measured on running text. The task check asks what a user asks: does a model with the bricks answer as many questions correctly as the 16-bit model? fdec_task_acc.py scores every cell on the same items, so each comparison is paired.

GSM8K is 1,319 grade-school math problems. The prompt holds eight worked examples, decoding is greedy, and an answer is correct when the first number after the answer marker equals the reference. A completion with no answer marker counts as wrong. MMLU is 14,042 four-choice questions with five examples per subject; the answer is the choice letter with the largest logit. A certified-head cell computes the logits of the last position in float32, which is the token the certificate returns. The other cells take the largest 16-bit logit, so the two can differ where the two largest logits tie within the 16-bit rounding step. Every table has the certified head alone and in the full stack. The asymmetric KV cells cast the values to FP8 directly, as the matrix does.

All runs are in bfloat16. Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen2.5-7B ran at knlp commit 4aa007a2c on one RTX 4090 with PyTorch 2.10 and transformers 5.3.0, in batches of up to 16 and 8 prompts. Qwen3-8B ran at the same commit on one Radeon Pro W7900 with PyTorch 2.9.1 and transformers 4.57.1, in batches of 32 prompts for GSM8K and up to 16 for MMLU. An earlier GSM8K run of Qwen2.5-7B serves as a control below. It ran on the W7900 at dd3a68306, with the certified-head cell at 0d5fe72b6, in batches of 32 prompts and with one FP8 scale per value tensor.

The 16-bit references are close to the published scores of the models. The MMLU accuracy of Llama-3.2-3B averaged over the 57 subjects is 57.95%, and its model card reports 58 with five examples. Qwen2.5-7B answers 74.25% of the MMLU items, or 75.46% averaged over the subjects, and the Qwen2.5 report gives 74.2 with five examples. It solves 81.1% of GSM8K with the eight examples used here; the report gives 85.4% with four examples of its own. Qwen3-8B answers 74.96% of the MMLU items, or 76.88% averaged over the subjects, and the Qwen3 report gives 76.89 for its base model. It solves 88.9% of GSM8K; the report gives 89.84 for the base model with four examples.

The test is the Wald test of equal accuracy of the cells on paired items (Bhapkar's test); its degrees of freedom are the number of cells minus one. The range is the accuracy of the highest cell minus the accuracy of the lowest cell.

Four tables show no difference among their cells. The two GSM8K tables of the Qwen models do, with p 0.007 and p 0.003; after a correction for the six tables these are 0.04 and 0.02. The value for Qwen2.5-7B is at the limit: Cochran's Q test, which assumes that all pairs of cells depend on each other equally, gives 0.011 for it, or 0.06 after the correction, and 0.0005 for Qwen3-8B. In both tables the largest difference is between two cells with bricks, and the 16-bit cell is between them. On Qwen2.5-7B the asymmetric cache alone solves 2.35 points more than the certified head alone: of the 65 problems that only one of the two cells solves, it solves 48. On Qwen3-8B int8 weights alone solve 1.82 points more than the asymmetric cache alone: of the 42 problems that only one of the two solves, they solve 33. The asymmetric cache is the highest cell of one table and the lowest cell of the other.

At these p-values the sample of problems is an unlikely cause of the two ranges. The test does not exclude that cause, and it does not tell what the cause is. A cell is one set of rounded numbers on one GPU. The test is evidence that two such sets differ in GSM8K accuracy on the test problems; it does not show that a brick is the cause. On MMLU the control of the first loss below gives the scale for one cell: four roundings of the same pair of bricks span 0.33 points, from 74.04% to 74.37%, which is more than the 0.24 points between the lowest and the highest cell of that table. The intervals in the tables cover the sample of items and not the choice of rounding. On GSM8K, a difference of 1 to 2 points between two cells of a Qwen model is therefore not, alone, evidence about a brick.

A control for these two tables runs the cells in question on other GSM8K problems, which are training problems that are not prompt examples: the 16-bit cell, the certified head and the asymmetric cache on 3,957 problems for Qwen2.5-7B, and the same cells with int8 weights on 2,638 problems for Qwen3-8B. The rule for the test was published before the cells with the asymmetric cache ran. If the test rejects equal accuracy on the other problems and the same cells are the lowest and the highest, the differences belong to the cells. If it rejects equal accuracy with other cells at the two ends, the cells differ and their order depends on the set of problems. If the test finds no difference there, the differences of the test tables are not reproduced.

An earlier run gives a second view, which was not planned as a control. It scored the same three cells on the test problems with other arithmetic. The test finds no difference among the cells there either (p 0.27). The asymmetric cache is 0.61 points below the 16-bit cell in that run, with an interval that contains zero. Its high result in the task table repeats neither on the same problems with other arithmetic nor on other problems. The certified head is below the 16-bit cell in both runs of the test problems, by 1.29 and 0.91 points, and 0.28 points above it on the other problems. The other problems are training problems, and the model may have seen them. For the certified head the evidence therefore cannot tell the sample of test problems from a loss that exists only on problems the model has not seen. The next subsection gives that control and the rules for the single cells whose interval against the 16-bit model excludes zero.

The four intervals that exclude zero

With 30 comparisons at the 95% level, one or two intervals are expected to exclude zero when no cell differs from the 16-bit model. Four do: two losses and two gains. If the comparisons were independent, four or more would occur in about one set of 16. They are not independent: the cells of one table share the 16-bit cell, and the two gains are in one table. With a Bonferroni correction over the 30 comparisons none of the four is significant: the smallest p-value becomes 0.12. Three of the four are in the two GSM8K tables whose cells differ among themselves in the test above. Each of the four has a control run. The controls of the two losses are done, and neither reproduces its loss. The controls of the two gains are running.

The two gains are in the GSM8K table of Qwen3-8B. int8 weights solve 90.30% of the problems against 88.93% for the 16-bit model, with 27 problems gained and 9 lost (p 0.004). The certified head alone solves 89.76%, with 14 gained and 3 lost (p 0.013). A second run of the 16-bit cell on the same GPU gives the same outcome on all 1,319 problems, so the gains are not a variation of the reference from run to run.

The two cells are not independent evidence. They share the 16-bit cell, and 9 of the 14 problems that the certified head gains are among the 27 that int8 weights gain; about 3 would be common if the two sets were independent. The gains do not add. The full stack has both bricks and solves 89.39%, with 20 problems gained and 14 lost. It is 1.29 points below the sum of the differences of its three bricks alone, with a 95% interval from 2.59 points below to 0.01 points above. In this table the outcome of 65 problems differs among the six cells. The 16-bit cell solves 25 of the 65, and the five cells with bricks solve 19, 31, 32, 36 and 43.

Two readings fit these counts. In the first, a difference belongs to one set of rounded numbers: this set of int8 weights solves more of the problems that the model solves by a narrow margin, this FP8 cast of the values solves fewer, and another set would land elsewhere. In the second, a brick raises GSM8K accuracy on this model. Rounding the weights is not expected to raise accuracy, and the other models do not show it on this task: int8 weights are 0.3 points above the 16-bit model on Llama-3.2-3B and 0.15 points below it on Qwen2.5-7B. On MMLU the same weights are level with the 16-bit model on Qwen3-8B: 74.94% against 74.96%, with 63 questions lost and 60 gained. For the certified head a gain has a possible cause, because the float32 logits are the more exact ones. But the same head is level with the 16-bit model on the MMLU questions of this model, 74.98% against 74.96% with 18 questions lost and 21 gained, and it is 1.29 points below on the GSM8K test problems of Qwen2.5-7B. The task tables alone do not tell the two readings apart.

Three controls test the gains. The first runs the 16-bit cell, the certified head, the asymmetric cache and int8 weights on the 2,638 other GSM8K problems. It runs on the RTX 4090, so it changes the problems and the GPU. The second runs the 16-bit cell, the certified head and int8 weights on the test problems on that GPU. If the first control does not reproduce a gain, the second tells if the gain belongs to the test problems or to the arithmetic of the W7900. The third runs int8 weights with two other group sizes on the W7900, which round the same weights to other grids. The rules were set before the runs. A gain is not reproduced if the interval on the other problems contains zero. A gain of the certified head that returns on the other problems, with an interval that excludes zero, is a property of the head on this model. A gain of int8 weights is a property of the brick on this model only if it returns on the other problems and with both other group sizes. If it returns on the other problems and not with the other group sizes, it belongs to one set of int8 weights. The first control is running, and the second is in the queue.

The third control is done, and its result is mixed. With group size 64 the gain does not return: int8 weights solve 89.01% of the test problems against 88.93% for the 16-bit model, with 10 problems lost and 11 gained. The difference is 0.08 points, with a 95% interval from 0.61 points below to 0.76 points above. 07 / What the measurements corrected

Two results did not survive a stricter measurement.

Each stricter measurement on this page found an error in an earlier result. Both corrections are recorded here, because they are the reason for the rules of the method.

A published byte saving was in-sample

The certified output head bounds every logit with a low-rank shadow of the head, plus a residual term for the part of the hidden state outside the shadow's basis. The earlier measurement built a rank-1280 basis from the same 600 to 1,500 hidden states that it then scored. A basis fitted to so few states contains them, so the residual was near zero and the decoder read about a quarter of the head.

The first held-out run of the matrix built the basis from 65,408 train positions and scored 8,176 test positions. The same certificate then opened every vocabulary block and read 118% of the dense head. The token was exact in both cases. Only the byte count was wrong, and only a held-out measurement could show it.

This is the reason for the rule that a brick counts only after the matrix measures it held-out and in combination.

The first matrix rounded attention scores to 16 bits

The first version of this matrix gave the 16-bit Qwen2.5-7B model a perplexity of 9.84. The correct value is 8.44. The task-accuracy run exposed the error: the same 16-bit model solved only 18% of the GSM8K problems.

The harness replaces the attention function of the model, because the asymmetric cache changes how values are read. For the unmodified model it called the plain attention function of the transformers library. That function rounds the query-key scores to the model data type before the softmax. A 16-bit float (bfloat16) keeps 8 significant bits, so a score near 700 moves in steps of 4. Qwen2.5-7B has a mean absolute score of 713 in its last layer, and the rounding moves a score there by 1.4 on average. A fused attention kernel accumulates the scores in float32 and does not have this error. The fused kernel is the default path of the library and of a serving engine.

Every cell now computes attention with float32 scores and a float32 value sum, the 16-bit model included. The asymmetric cache cell therefore differs from its reference only in the precision of the values. All three models were measured again. Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen3-8B moved by less than 0.03 in perplexity. Qwen2.5-7B changed in two ways. Its 16-bit perplexity is 8.44 and not 9.84. And it is no longer the weakest model: the first matrix showed int8 weights changing 8.3% of its predicted tokens, and with correct attention they change 2.4%, close to the 1.7% and 2.0% of the other two models. With scores rounded to 16 bits, a small change to the model moves many scores across a rounding step, which is the likely source of the 8.3%.

08 / Speed

What has been timed so far.

The matrix shows that the bricks agree in what they compute. Speed needs each brick in a real kernel. Two measurements exist: two bricks together in a serving engine, and the certified head alone against the dense head.

Two bricks in one serving engine

Packed int4 weights and the asymmetric K16/V8 cache ran in one vLLM and FlashInfer process on Qwen3-8B, with 8,192 input tokens at batch 32 on one H100. Request throughput was 3.45% higher than the same weights with a 16-bit cache, and the tested quality checks passed. With dense 16-bit weights the same cache change gave 3.55%. The cache brick therefore keeps its gain when the weights are packed. The asymmetric KV results give the controls.

That measurement counts whole requests, and an 8,192-token prompt with 128 output tokens is mostly prefill. It does not test the decode byte model on this page. The weights in it are int4 and not the int8 of the matrix.

The certified head on unseen hidden states

On hidden states it has not seen, the certified head runs at 0.99 to 1.28 times the speed of the dense head in the mean, and on three of the four models its 95th-percentile token takes about 1.8 times as long. The earlier figures of 1.97 and 1.85 times came from the in-sample shadow and do not describe a deployed head.

fdec_cert_latency.py builds the full-rank shadow from WikiText-2 train hidden states. It then times 1,000 WikiText-2 test positions, one token at a time, against the dense 16-bit head on the same GPU. The timed variant makes one fused fetch of the vocabulary blocks with the highest bounds, then checks the certificate once. A token that does not certify falls back to the dense head.

The variant that reads the fewest bytes is not the fastest. Opening one block at a time until the certificate closes reads 51% to 56% of the head, which is the figure in the matrix. Its mean speed is only 0.72 to 1.16 times the dense head, because each block costs a kernel launch. A dense head at batch 1 takes 0.3 to 2 ms, so there is little time to win, and a token that falls back pays for the screen and for the dense product.

The script repeats the in-sample setup on the same GPU. There the fused variant is 1.56 to 1.78 times faster in the mean on the three larger models. That reproduces the size of the earlier figures, so the difference is the shadow and not the kernel.

"Exact" on this page means equal to the argmax of float32 logits. The block-by-block variant certified every token and returned that argmax for every token on all four models. The dense 16-bit head rounds its logits to 8 significant bits, which makes ties among the top tokens: it returned the float32 argmax on 94.8% to 98.6% of the tokens.

09 / Run it

One defconfig runs the matrix.

# Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B, all eight cells each
make defconfig-fdec
make

# the same eight cells on Qwen2.5-0.5B on the CPU, under three minutes
make defconfig-fdec-smoke
make

# optional: GSM8K and MMLU for the same bricks, hours per model on one GPU
make fdec-tasks
make fdec-report

make runs scripts/fdec_compose.py once per model. It writes one result file per model and a REPORT.md table under results/fdec, and it exits non-zero if any cell does not compose. The report gives the interaction term of each model with its 95% interval. A model with a result file is skipped, so an interrupted run resumes. The 7B model needs a GPU with 24 GiB of memory; each model takes two to five minutes. The smoke configuration checks the harness, not the numbers: its calibration set is small.

make fdec-tasks runs scripts/fdec_task_acc.py once per model: GSM8K and MMLU for the 16-bit model, each enabled brick alone and the stack, on the same items. It is not part of the default target, because it takes about four hours for the 3B model on one RTX 4090. make fdec-report then adds the task tables to the report and fails if a cell is significantly below the 16-bit model. The test is the exact McNemar test on the items that changed, with a Bonferroni correction over the cells of one model and task, at 0.05. When a cell differs from the 16-bit model on the test split, you can check it on other problems: scripts/fdec_task_acc.py --gsm-split train scores the 7,465 GSM8K training problems that are not prompt examples.

The options are in Kconfig.fdec. CONFIG_FDEC_MODELS takes a space-separated list of Hugging Face model names. Each brick has its own switch, and the harness measures every subset of the enabled bricks.

Adding a brick

A new brick enters the stack in four steps. State the byte pool it reduces and its result alone on a held-out evaluation. Add it to fdec_compose.py, either as a transform that changes the model or its attention, or as a measurement that checks an exactness claim on the changed model. Add its factor to the byte model. Then run the matrix. The brick is in when every cell that contains it composes on at least two model families.

10 / Limits

What these results do not show.

The matrix emulates the bricks in PyTorch. Weights are rounded in place, values pass through a direct FP8 cast, attention runs with float32 arithmetic, and the certified decode is replayed against float32 logits. This shows that the bricks do not conflict in what they compute. It does not measure speed, and "bytes saved" is a model of bytes read, not a latency.

The perplexity evaluation is WikiText-2 only, on 8,192 tokens per model. The task tables are complete. The control runs for two of the four comparisons whose interval excludes zero are not finished. The cartridge cells cover one model and two documents, and their loss level is in-sample. Linear-attention models are not in the matrix. No single serving engine runs all four bricks today.
